Charlotte Brontë's voice, Creative Writing 2018

I was beyond tired: I hadn’t gotten a full night’s rest ever since I could remember and it has definitely shown with the prominent dark bags that have accumulated under my eyes over the years, showcasing my lifetime of exhaustion like a hunter showing off the carcass of his first kill.  

I reached the entrance of my school not knowing whether to go in or turn back because everything had already started to flicker: the image of the neatly constructed brick walls of the school painted a light brown flickered to crumbling bricks of faded red; the entrance composed of three glass doors flickered to a single wooden one; and the students maneuvering around me to walk through the doors flickered into wiry versions of themselves in clothing worn by people from the 1920s. I saw two different images of the same things – one from the past, the other in the present – constantly morphing into each other, each trying to hold my attention like a baby latching onto its mother’s leg as she tries to walk away. 

“Mia, are you okay?” I tried to look at my teacher, but the contrast of his morphing appearance standing in front of the whiteboard flickering into a black one was just too much for me. 

“Yeah.” And with that, I gave him the courtesy of pretending to do his homework. The constantly morphing floor, desks, and chairs made the voice in my head scream out, and I had to bite my tongue to keep myself from actually doing it. 
 
I went to the library to escape the migraine-bearing cacophony of the cafeteria. And I began to write, translating all my raw emotions into words and releasing my most deeply suppressed thoughts onto paper. It was so liberating to me, because out of everything I did in my life, writing was the only thing that kept me grounded; in a life filled with unpredictable, flickering variables, writing was my only constant. 

My hallucinations of the past morphing into the present never gave me a break until I started to write. Out of everything in my life, writing was the only thing I could control. After each headache-inducing day of school, my notebook always laid wide open on my desk, waiting for me to write in it like a baby bird waiting to be fed. 

As I recapped my pen and wiped the tears from my face, I felt a wave of tranquility wash over me like the tides of a beach washing over its shore. I knew it was only the calmness before the storm, but I still felt comforted by my knowledge of what I could control because no one could take it from me.
